Song Youan is twenty-six when a crash puts him in a coma. In a desolate place he meets a man called River, who tells him he has to pass at least one of three trials to get back. Each takes him to a moment in his life and asks him to choose again.

The trials give Youan a clear way to pass, but once he is inside a memory, it’s harder to see where the right answer is meant to be. The chance to go back might mean a few more words with somebody he didn’t know how to talk to at the time. Knowing what happens afterwards still leaves him with the work of saying those words. It can make that first sentence harder.

There are two kinds of time to keep track of as you read. At the border, there is the urgent question of whether Youan will wake up. In a memory, the story can take the time it needs over a line of conversation or a small action. After a trial, it’s worth looking back to see which of those quiet moments begins to matter to you once you know there was a deadline hanging over it.

Teen readers could choose one moment and come up with two ways of making sense of it: Youan’s explanation at the time and a second that the reader can imagine. See how far you can follow each reading before it changes how you understand his choice.

It’s a book worth taking slowly. When Youan goes back into a memory, notice what he knows now and what he couldn’t see at the time. The story involves a crash, a coma and choices about life and death.
